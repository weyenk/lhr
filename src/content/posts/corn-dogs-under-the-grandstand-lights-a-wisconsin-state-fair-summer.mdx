steps:
  - "Pat the hot dogs completely dry with paper towels — this is what keeps the batter from sliding off later. Skewer each one lengthwise with a wooden stick, leaving a few inches to hold onto."
  - "Dust the skewered hot dogs lightly and evenly with the 1/4 cup of flour, tapping off the excess. Set them aside on a plate."
  - "In a large bowl, whisk together the cornmeal, flour, sugar, baking powder, and salt."
  - "In a separate bowl, whisk the buttermilk, egg, and 2 tablespoons of vegetable oil until smooth. Pour into the dry ingredients and stir just until combined into a thick, scoopable batter. Let it rest for 10 minutes."
  - "Pour the batter into a tall drinking glass or narrow jar — deep enough to submerge a full hot dog in one dip."
  - "Heat the frying oil in a heavy pot or Dutch oven to 350°F (175°C)."
  - "Working one at a time, dip each floured hot dog into the batter, turning to coat it completely and letting the excess drip off for a second or two."
  - "Carefully lower the battered hot dog into the hot oil. Fry 2-3 at a time so the temperature doesn't drop, turning occasionally, until deep golden brown all over, about 3-4 minutes."
  - "Lift out with tongs and drain on a wire rack or paper towels. Let cool for a minute before handing them out — the inside holds heat like nothing else."
  - "Serve hot, with plenty of yellow mustard and ketchup for dunking."

Every summer, the Wisconsin State Fair meant the same four of us in the same order: my dad in front reading the map like it was going to change, my mom keeping a hand near my collar, my little brother already asking about the next ride before we'd finished the one we were on, and me, somewhere in the middle, trying to memorize the whole day so it wouldn't end.

We'd start with the rides, because that's where the energy was highest and the lines were shortest, and end up sticky and a little dizzy by lunchtime. Then came the barns — cool and dim after the midway sun, full of the particular hush that happens around large animals. My brother would plant himself in front of every pig pen like he was cataloguing them. I liked the horses best, the big gentle ones with names taped to their stalls, breathing slow and huge.

By late afternoon we'd find our way to the grandstand for the horse show, and that's when the corn dogs showed up — one for each of us, wrapped in a paper sleeve, still too hot to bite into right away. We'd sit on the bleachers with them balanced on our knees, watching the horses circle below, waiting for the batter to cool just enough.

![A family of corn dogs lined up on paper trays with mustard swirls, ready to eat](https://placehold.co/1600x1000?text=Corn+Dogs+Lineup)

I've made a lot of fair food at home over the years trying to chase that exact feeling, and corn dogs are the one that actually gets there. Something about the crackle of the cornmeal crust giving way to the snap of the hot dog underneath — it's not fancy, and it was never supposed to be. It's the taste of sitting still for the first time all day, brother next to me, horses in front of us, nowhere else to be.

**Pro Tip:** Keep the oil temperature steady at 350°F between batches — if it dips too low the batter soaks up oil instead of crisping, and if it climbs too high the outside browns before the inside cooks through. A clip-on candy thermometer takes the guesswork out of it.

## Wisconsin State Fair Corn Dogs

**Tips & Variations:**

- No wooden skewers on hand? Sturdy bamboo chopsticks work in a pinch.
- For a thinner, crispier coating, thin the batter with a tablespoon or two more buttermilk.
- Mini corn dogs are a fun variation for kids — cut the hot dogs into thirds and reduce the fry time to about 2 minutes.

However you make them, save the first bite for right off the fryer, standing up, before you've even found a seat.
